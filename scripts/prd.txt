Project OverviewTesting: Use Playwright for end-to-end tests and Jest (or Vitest) for unit/integration. In E2E, stub tile and geocoding network requests, and assert graceful fallbacks when map keys are missing.Project Overview

The Sustainable Eco-Friendly Digital Nomads Directory is a web platform that helps remote workers (digital nomads) find sustainable, eco-friendly venues and services in Thailand. The product addresses the need for environmentally conscious travelers to easily discover coworking spaces, accommodations, cafés, and other services that align with sustainable practices. By providing verified information on eco-friendly features (like renewable energy use or community initiatives) and digital nomad amenities (reliable Wi-Fi, workspaces, etc.), the platform empowers users to make informed choices that support sustainable travel. Built with Next.js (React) and a modern tech stack, the directory will deliver a fast, intuitive user experience while ensuring content is up-to-date through a headless CMS backend. The refactoring project’s goal is to comprehensively define and implement all core features of the website – from content discovery to user engagement – in a structured, maintainable way. Key Goals and Objectives:
Comprehensive Directory: Curate a thorough list of eco-friendly venues across Thailand (e.g. green hotels, co-working hubs, cafes) with detailed information on their services and sustainability practices.
Empower Digital Nomads: Enable users to easily find places that meet both their remote work needs and eco-values, improving their experience living and working in Thailand.
Promote Sustainability: Highlight venues’ sustainable initiatives (solar power, recycling programs, etc.) and foster an eco-conscious community by featuring user reviews and ratings on sustainability.
Easy Content Management: Allow content managers/admins to update listings and moderate user contributions via an integrated CMS (Sanity), ensuring information remains accurate and moderated without direct code changes.
Scalable & Maintainable Platform: Refactor the codebase to use best practices (modular architecture, design system, latest Next.js features) for long-term scalability, easy addition of new features, and performance optimization.
Target Audience
The primary audience is digital nomads relocating to or traveling in Thailand, who are seeking sustainable living and working options. These users are typically remote workers or entrepreneurs, often tech-savvy and socially conscious, who need reliable infrastructure (internet, work-friendly spaces) and prefer businesses that minimize environmental impact. They will use the directory to find places to stay, work, eat, and socialize that align with their lifestyle. Key characteristics and needs of this audience include:
Remote Work Needs: High-speed internet, comfortable work areas, and communities of other nomads are important. They will filter for features like Wi-Fi quality, coworking facilities, and events for networking.
Eco-Conscious Values: They actively seek out accommodations or cafes with green practices (e.g. renewable energy, plastic-free policies). The platform should clearly showcase each listing’s eco-friendly attributes and certifications.
Local Exploration: As newcomers to Thailand’s cities, they need guidance on where to go. City-specific information (climate, cost, culture) and highlights of sustainable attractions or services in each city will be valuable.
Convenience & Trust: They prefer an easy-to-use, fast site with trustworthy information. User reviews and verified data (e.g. “verified by our team” badges) will help build trust that the listings are reliable and community-endorsed.
Secondary audiences include local sustainable business owners (who may use the platform to reach nomads by getting their venues listed) and eco-conscious travelers visiting Thailand for shorter stays. However, the product will primarily cater to the needs of digital nomads in terms of features and content emphasis.
Scope and Constraints
Geographic Scope: The directory will focus exclusively on Thailand. All listings are located within Thailand’s cities and regions. Major nomad hubs like Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Phuket, etc., will be covered. (Expansion to other countries or global coverage is out-of-scope for now but can be considered in the future.)
Content Scope: Listings will include sustainable businesses relevant to nomads: co-working spaces, eco-friendly cafés/restaurants, “green” accommodations (hotels, hostels, co-living spaces), and possibly other services (e.g. sustainable tour operators or community hubs) if applicable. Each listing is curated to meet a baseline of sustainability criteria (e.g. having certain eco-friendly practices) so that the directory maintains a clear focus. Non-eco-friendly or unrelated businesses will not be listed.
Content Source: All listing content is internally managed through Sanity CMS. Content is added and edited by the project team (or approved contributors) via the Sanity Studio interface. There are no external scraped or third-party feeds populating the listings – quality is maintained through manual curation and verification. (User-generated content is limited to reviews and suggestions, which are subject to moderation.)
If geocoding is required, use Google Geocoding API or OpenCage during data entry or in the submission workflow to persist lat/long. Initial datasets likely already include coordinates (placeholders exist for location).

User Experience & User Journeys
The product should offer a simple, intuitive user experience that caters to the journey of a digital nomad discovering sustainable options in Thailand. Below are key user personas and their typical flows through the product:
Persona 1 – “Explorer” (Digital Nomad User): Alice is a remote software developer planning to spend a few months in Chiang Mai. She visits the site to research where she can work and live sustainably. Her journey: she lands on the homepage and uses the search bar to input “Chiang Mai coworking”. She filters results to show only Coworking Spaces in Chiang Mai, and sorts by rating. She clicks on a listing to view the Listing Detail page with rich information (description, amenities, eco-friendly practices, user reviews, and a map). She reads that the space uses solar panels and has a composting program – aligning with her values. She also sees other digital nomad features listed, like “24/7 access and community events”. After comparing a few listings, she marks one as a favorite (for future reference, requiring her to create an account). She then navigates to the City page for Chiang Mai, where she reads an overview of Chiang Mai’s sustainability score and highlights


 – e.g., good air quality part of year, many organic markets – which reinforces her decision. She finally signs up and leaves a review on the coworking space after visiting it, to help future users.
Persona 2 – “Eco Business Owner”: Ben runs a small eco-friendly guesthouse in Phuket. He wants to attract digital nomads. On the site, he finds the “Add Listing” (Submit a Listing) page or call-to-action. Ben fills out a form with his venue details (name, category = Accommodation, address, descriptions, sustainable practices, images). The form is submitted to the site administrators as a “pending” listing for review. An admin later reviews and verifies the info (possibly contacting Ben for proof of certain eco practices) and then publishes the listing so it appears on the website. Ben can then see his guesthouse listed; in the future he may claim the listing as the owner to update details or respond to reviews. (Note: The owner interaction is limited in MVP; direct editing by owners might be introduced in later phases via a special account role.)
Persona 3 – “Admin/Content Curator”: Cara is an administrator for the site. She uses the Sanity CMS to input new listings and ensure data consistency. When Ben’s submission comes in, Cara receives a notification (or checks a moderation queue in the CMS or admin dashboard). She verifies the content meets guidelines (sustainability criteria) and sets the listing’s status to “Published”. She can also mark exceptional venues as “Featured” so they show up prominently on the homepage

. Cara regularly monitors incoming user reviews; if any review is inappropriate or spammy, she can remove it via an admin tool or CMS. She also updates the “Last Verified Date” on listings periodically to indicate information freshness

. Additionally, Cara writes blog articles (e.g., “Top 5 Eco Retreats in Thailand”) via the CMS, which appear in the Blog section of the site, helping drive engagement and SEO.
General User Flow Notes: The site’s navigation should make it effortless to jump between high-level browsing (by city or category) and specific details. For example, from the homepage, a user can select a city to see all listings there, or use search filters to narrow down by category or eco-tag across all of Thailand. Once on a listing detail, the page may suggest similar listings (e.g., other eco-friendly cafés in the same city) to encourage exploration. A consistent header menu will provide quick access to key sections: Home, Search, Cities, Blog, and user account options (login/profile) if applicable. Throughout the journey, the interface must remain clear, with no ambiguity in labels or instructions – e.g., using icons and text to denote features (🌱 for eco features, 💻 for digital nomad facilities) and tooltips or info modals for any potentially unclear terms. The tone of content and labels should be friendly and straightforward, suitable for an international audience.
Core Features and Requirements
1. Listing Catalog & Discovery
Home Page: The homepage will serve as a starting point for discovery. It should prominently feature a search bar (allowing keyword search for cities, venue names, categories) and call-to-action buttons for common tasks (e.g., “Browse by City”, “Suggest a Listing”). The home page can highlight Featured Listings – a few top-rated or editor-picked venues that exemplify eco-friendly nomad spots – to immediately show the value of the directory. It may also show a teaser of major cities (with an image and sustainability score snippet) and/or latest blog posts. The design is welcoming and quickly orients users on what the site offers. City Pages: For each key city (e.g., Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Phuket), there will be a dedicated page. This page displays the city’s name, an image, and a brief descriptive overview emphasizing its appeal to digital nomads and eco-friendliness. Example: “Chiang Mai – A mountainous city known for its digital nomad community and improving air quality. Sustainability initiatives include bike-sharing and organic farms…”. The page will show the city’s Sustainability Score (0–100) which is a summary indicator of how eco-friendly the city is

. It will also list a few eco highlights (bullet points like “Excellent air quality in winter months” or “Has city-wide recycling program”) drawn from the CMS

. Below this intro, the Listings in that City are displayed. Users can filter or sort this city-specific list by category (coworking, cafe, accommodation, etc.) or by rating. A small city map may be present showing pins for the listings in that city for geographic context. City pages help users narrow focus to one location and see all options there at a glance. Listings Index & Categories: Users can browse all listings via either a global search or by selecting a category. The platform will support broad categories such as Coworking Spaces, Cafés & Restaurants, Accommodations, etc. There isn’t a separate static page per category initially (since Thailand-wide category listing can be achieved via search filters), but the UI will allow filtering by category easily. For instance, a user could filter to see all “Accommodations” in Thailand or in a specific city. The category of each listing is stored and displayed (e.g., on a listing card it might say “Category: Coworking Space”). This categorization is used for filtering logic. If feasible, the site can provide quick category links (e.g., “Explore Coworking Spaces”, which effectively performs a search filter for that category). Search & Advanced Filtering: The platform provides a powerful search function to sift through listings. Users can search by keywords (matching listing name, description, etc.) with support for full-text search on relevant fields. They can also apply filters to refine results: by Category, by City/Location, and by specific tags or features. Notably, users can filter by Eco-Tags (sustainability features) and by Digital Nomad Features, to find listings that meet certain criteria. For example, a user could filter for venues that have the “solar-powered” tag, or those marked with “fast Wi-Fi”. The system supports multi-filter combinations (e.g., show cafes in Bangkok with “Plastic-Free” tag). This advanced filtering capability is a core feature

. The UI for filters will likely be a sidebar or dropdown on search results with checkboxes or multi-select inputs for each filter category. Search results should update in real-time or on submission of the filters, showing matching listings as a list of cards with key info. Each result card will typically show a thumbnail image, name, category, location (city), a short description or highlight, and maybe an icon summary of its eco/nomad features (like a Wi-Fi icon if it has great Wi-Fi, a leaf icon if it’s notably green). Users can click on any result to go to the detailed page.
Performance Consideration: The search and filter operations should be efficient even as the number of listings grows. Initially, with Thailand-only data, this might be a few hundred listings, which Sanity’s querying or a simple in-memory filter can handle. If the data scales up, we may integrate a search indexing service (like Algolia or similar) for faster full-text search. For now, the implementation will likely use Sanity’s GROQ queries or API routes to filter data on the backend. We will ensure that search is fast and yields relevant results (e.g., boosting exact matches in title higher

, and possibly using additional keywords field to improve search relevance

).
Geo-Filtering/Map: The search interface may include an option to view results on a map. Using the integrated map component, users can see pins of listings matching their search, which helps visualize proximity. (For example, a nomad could see which co-working is near a certain neighborhood.) Each listing in Sanity has a geolocation (latitude/longitude) stored so that it can be placed on the map

. The map view is pan/zoomable and might allow filtering by drawing an area (nice-to-have, can be later). At minimum, on the listing detail page there will be a small map showing that listing’s location with a marker.
Listing Detail Page: This is the core page for each venue/listing, aggregating all information a user needs about that venue. Requirements for the listing detail page:
Basic Info: Display the name of the venue, category, and exact address (with a link to open in Google Maps or another navigation app). Also show the city and perhaps neighborhood. If the user is logged in and has certain roles, we might show additional info like an “Edit this listing” link for owners/admins (permission-based).
Images Gallery: Show the primary image prominently (hero image/banner) and allow the user to scroll through a gallery of additional images. All images have appropriate alt text for accessibility

. Users should be able to click to enlarge images or view them in a lightbox.
Description: Show both a short summary (couple of lines) and a longer description of the venue. The short description gives a quick overview (e.g., “Cozy co-working space in old town with a community garden”), while the detailed description provides in-depth information like history, mission, sustainability initiatives, etc. These come from the CMS fields for each listing.
Eco-Friendly Practices: Highlight the listing’s sustainability features. This can include a list of Eco-Tags associated with the listing (for example: Solar Powered, Recycling Program, Locally Sourced Food). These tags are stored per listing

 and should be displayed as badges or icons. If the listing has a special write-up about its eco practices (“Eco Notes Detailed” field), that text is shown as well (perhaps in an expandable section for those interested in more background).
Digital Nomad Features: Also display the digital nomad-friendly features of the venue. This might be a list of amenities important to remote workers: e.g., High-Speed WiFi, Dedicated Desks, Air Conditioning, Community Events, Coffee & Snacks, etc. These come from the listing’s digital_nomad_features array

. This info helps a user quickly assess if the place suits working needs.
Category-Specific Details: Depending on the category of the listing, show additional relevant details:
For Co-working Spaces: list operating hours, types of pricing plans (day pass, monthly, etc.), and specific amenities (e.g., conference room, standing desks)


.
For Cafés: show hours, an indication of price or typical spend, any menu highlights (especially sustainable or vegan options), and notes on WiFi reliability


.
For Accommodations: show the type (hotel, hostel, co-living), approximate price range per night, available room types, and key amenities (e.g., kitchen access, co-working area, free bikes)


.
These fields ensure the user gets a complete picture tailored to the type of venue. They will be presented clearly, possibly in a key-value list or icon set (for example, a bed icon with “Private rooms and dorms available” for accommodations).
Map Location: Embed a small interactive map (using Leaflet with OpenStreetMap data) showing the venue’s exact location with a pin. This helps users see where it is and what’s nearby. The map should be zoomed appropriately to show local context. If multiple listings are in view, potentially show those too (though for a single listing page, typically one pin is fine).
Reviews & Ratings: The listing page will include a user reviews section where travelers can read feedback from others and see an average rating. At the top of this section, show an average star rating (1–5 stars) if reviews exist, and the count of reviews. Below, list individual reviews including the reviewer’s name (or anonymized handle), the rating score (1–5 stars), date, and their comments. Reviews are stored in the CMS and associated with listings


. The UI should perhaps show 3-5 reviews by default and allow expanding to view more or paginating if there are many. If no reviews yet, display a message like “Be the first to review this place” with a call-to-action button for logged-in users to add a review.
Add Review: A logged-in user can submit a new review from the listing page. This will involve a form with a star rating input (1 to 5) and a text box for comments. Upon submission, the review will either immediately display (if we allow instant posting) or go into a pending state for moderation (depending on moderation policy, likely the latter for safety). For MVP, reviews might be posted immediately but with ability for admin to remove if needed – however, given quality concerns, we might require admin approval in the workflow. We will clarify moderation in a later section. In any case, the review system needs to prevent spam and abuse (e.g., by requiring login and possibly limiting one review per user per listing).
Favorites & Bookmarking: If a user is logged in, they should see an option (e.g., a heart icon) to favorite or bookmark the listing. Clicking it will save that listing to their personal list of favorites. This helps users build a shortlist of places they are interested in. (The icon should toggle state when favorited, and perhaps a toast or message confirms “Added to your favorites”.) Favoriting is a quick action and should be reflected in the user’s profile or a “Favorites” page.
Call to Action & External Links: The listing detail page should help the user act on the information. For example, provide a link to the venue’s website or booking page (open in new tab) if available, so the user can make a reservation or learn more. Also include a contact method – this could be an email or phone number for the venue (if provided in the CMS) or a “Contact Venue” button if we decide to facilitate contact via our site (not likely needed initially). If the venue offers online booking or we have an affiliate link, that can be integrated here in the future.
Meta Info: The page should also show meta details like “Last Verified: [date]” which indicates when the data was last confirmed by the team

. This builds trust that info is current. Additionally, if the listing was user-submitted, it might show “Submitted by [username]” and a note if it’s awaiting verification (“information not verified” warning if applicable). We will also indicate if the listing is Verified (a flag meaning our team has checked its sustainability claims) – for example, a badge or text “✅ Verified Eco-Friendly” if the verificationStatus is set to verified

.
Similar Listings: At the bottom of the listing page, the site will suggest Similar Listings that the user might be interested in. This could be implemented by showing other listings in the same city and category, or using a curated list from the CMS (there’s a field for similar listings references in the schema

). The purpose is to keep the user engaged and exploring other venues. For example, on a café listing page, it might show 2-3 other cafes in that city or region.
2. User Accounts & Profiles
The platform includes a user account system to enable personalized features (like adding reviews and favorites) and to differentiate access for various roles. We will implement authentication via email/password initially, using NextAuth.js (a robust auth library). Key requirements:
User Registration & Login: Visitors can create an account using their email and a password. The registration flow will include email verification (sending a confirmation link) to ensure valid sign-ups. Logging in will start a session so the user can perform account-related actions. We’ll provide standard Log In and Sign Up pages, plus support for password reset (in case a user forgets their password, they can request a reset link via email). All authentication flows must be secure (passwords stored hashed, etc.) and user-friendly with clear feedback on errors.
Social Login (Optional/Nice-to-have): In the future, we may allow login via Google, Facebook, or other OAuth providers to simplify onboarding. This is not mandatory for MVP, but the system (NextAuth) is capable of it, so design the auth in a way that can extend to social logins (e.g., buttons for “Continue with Google” can be present but it’s acceptable to add in a later phase).
User Roles and Permissions: The application will employ role-based access control using NextAuth session data. There are multiple user roles defined, each with different permissions

:
General User: A normal registered user (default role). Can create reviews, favorite listings, and manage their own profile. Cannot add new listings directly (except via a suggestion form) and cannot edit content not owned by them. No admin capabilities.
Moderator/Editor: A content editor role (trusted user or staff) who can log into an admin interface or CMS to manage content. Editors can approve or reject listings and reviews, edit listing details in the CMS, and generally ensure quality. They do not have full system rights like managing user accounts, but they handle content moderation.
Venue Owner: A special role for business owners. This role might be assigned when a user who owns a venue is verified. A venueOwner could be allowed to suggest edits to their listing or respond to reviews on their listing. (Initial implementation might not fully empower this role, but we plan the structure for it.) They have more access to their own listing’s data but not others.
Admin: Full administrators with access to all data and management tools. Admins can manage user roles, remove content, feature listings, etc. They have access to an admin dashboard if provided, or they can use the CMS for many tasks.
SuperAdmin: Possibly a higher-level admin (for example, the site owner/developer role) with rights to manage lower admins and perform dangerous operations. In practical terms, Admin and SuperAdmin are similar for product requirements; these roles ensure we can differentiate if needed (e.g., SuperAdmin can manage platform settings or have database access).
The system will enforce these permissions such that protected pages or actions are only accessible to the appropriate roles. For example, attempting to access the admin moderation page as a general user will show an “Unauthorized” error page (these NextAuth guard pages exist in the system

).
User Profile Management: Each user will have a basic profile page where they can edit personal information (name, avatar, bio if we allow). At minimum, they should be able to change their display name (or it could default from email before they set one) and upload a profile picture. They should also be able to update their password, and set preferences like opting into newsletters (if we have any) or choosing to enable dark mode (though dark mode could also just follow system setting automatically). If feasible, the profile can show their activity: e.g., list of reviews they’ve written, their favorite listings bookmarked, etc., for the user’s reference. This is mostly for their own use, but could be expanded into a public profile if the community aspect grows (not in initial scope to have public user profiles).
Favorites List: A logged-in user can view all the listings they have marked as favorite. This might be a simple page under their account (or a section on the profile page) showing a list of their bookmarked venues, with links to those listing pages. This feature helps users keep track of places they are interested in visiting. We will ensure that favorites persist in the user’s account (stored in the database) so they’re available on any device once logged in. Removing a favorite will also update this list immediately.
Notifications (Email): When a user registers, they receive a welcome/verification email. If they submit a listing suggestion or a review, they might receive a confirmation email (to acknowledge receipt). Also, if their suggested listing is approved, the system could send a notification email saying “Your listing has been published!” Similarly, password resets and other account actions will trigger emails. We will integrate an email service (like SendGrid or Resend) for these transactional emails. All emails should use a consistent template/branding and be written in clear language. (Real-time on-site notifications are not planned for MVP, aside from perhaps inline messages like “Your review has been submitted.” Email suffices for now for any needed alerts.)
Account Deletion and Data Export: In line with good practice (and likely legal compliance like GDPR), we should allow a user to delete their account and remove personal data. This would typically be a button in profile settings “Delete my account” which prompts confirmation. Deleting an account would remove their personal data from our databases (but perhaps keep their reviews as anonymous, or also delete them – we must decide policy). Similarly, we might allow a user to request an export of their data (though given minimal data, this might not be heavily used). These are nice-to-have features that could be added in later phases, but it’s good to note them as requirements for completeness.
3. Reviews & Ratings System
The ability for users to leave reviews is vital for community engagement and trust-building on the platform. The review system’s requirements include:
Posting Reviews: Logged-in users can add a review to any listing. A review consists of a star rating (1 to 5 stars) and a text comment. We will require at least a rating and a brief comment (e.g., minimum 20 characters) to ensure reviews are informative. The UI will clearly indicate the rating scale (1 star = poor, 5 stars = excellent) and ask users to focus their comments on the nomad and eco-friendly aspects (perhaps a note like “How was the Wi-Fi? Did you notice sustainable practices?” to guide feedback). When submitted, the review is time-stamped and associated with the user’s account (so we know the author).
Displaying Reviews: Reviews on a listing page will be listed in order (most recent first by default, or possibly highest rated first – but recency is usually better for temporal context). Each review display will show the reviewer’s name (or username), their rating with star icons, the date, and their comment text. If a review is lengthy, we might truncate and let users expand it. We might also summarize – e.g., show an overall average rating atop the listing page as mentioned, possibly broken down by aspect if we gather such data (for now, just an overall rating average).
Review Moderation: To maintain quality, the system will include moderation capabilities for reviews. Administrators (or assigned moderators) can remove or edit reviews that violate guidelines (e.g., offensive language, spam, irrelevant content). We will have usage guidelines in place (like “no hate speech, keep reviews relevant”) possibly presented when writing a review. Technically, a review could be stored with a field like status (approved/pending). For MVP, we might auto-approve all new reviews and rely on after-the-fact moderation (simply deleting or marking hidden any bad ones). In a later phase, we might implement a workflow where new reviews are flagged as “pending” and require an admin to approve before they become public (this ensures nothing bad goes live, but it’s more friction and requires admin attention). The PRD’s direction is to be determined by how much moderation overhead is acceptable initially. Given we want a high-quality directory, we lean toward either at least light automated spam filtering (e.g., check if content looks spammy) and/or community flagging (users can “Report” a review) in addition to admin oversight.
Rating Calculation: The average rating for each listing should be calculated and displayed. This can be done on the fly (each time a listing page loads, compute mean of all review scores) or precomputed and stored (e.g., update the listing’s data in CMS or a separate field whenever a new review is added). Simpler initial approach: compute on fly by querying the reviews. With the likely moderate volume of reviews, performance is fine. For display, we’ll show something like “4.5/5 ★ (based on 10 reviews)”. We should also handle edge cases: if no reviews, say “No reviews yet”; if only one, say “based on 1 review”.
Preventing Abuse: Each user can generally only post one review per listing (they can edit their review or post an updated one, but we don’t want one person spamming multiple reviews for the same place). Also, if a user hasn’t actually visited a place, they shouldn’t review (this is hard to enforce, but we might include a disclaimer or rely on community reporting bogus reviews). We will not implement complex verification (like purchase verification) for reviews at this time. However, to improve trust, perhaps an admin/editor could mark certain reviews as “verified visit” if they have evidence, but this is not core. A simpler mechanism: require that a user at least provides some profile info, and potentially if we detect suspicious activity (e.g., a brand new account posting many 5-star reviews for one venue), admins will investigate. In future, we could add features like upvoting reviews (was this review helpful?)

 or allowing owners to respond to reviews, but those are enhancements for later.
4. Listing Submission & Moderation
To grow the directory, users or business owners should be able to suggest new listings or updates to existing listings. Additionally, content moderation is needed to ensure quality and accuracy. Requirements:
Submit New Listing (Suggestion Form): The site will have a page (accessible via a prominent button like “Add a Listing” on the nav or footer) where anyone can fill out a form to suggest a new venue to add. The form fields will correspond to the listing data needed: e.g., Name, Category (dropdown of our allowed categories), City (possibly also a dropdown of Thai cities we focus on, plus an “Other” if needed), Address, short description, and a section to describe its eco-friendly practices (with maybe a checklist of common eco features or a text field). We will also allow an image upload (at least one photo of the venue) to give a visual. The user submitting should provide their contact info (if they are an owner or just a user who wants to suggest). On submission, this data is sent to our backend – likely creating a new entry in the CMS as status = “pending” (not published)

. Admins are then notified (via an email or by checking an admin page) about the new suggestion. The UI will show a success message like “Thank you! Your suggestion has been submitted and is pending review.”
Moderation Workflow for Listings: Admins or Editors will periodically review pending listings. They will verify the information: possibly checking the venue’s website or contacting the submitter. If it meets the criteria, they will change its status to Published so it goes live on the site

. If it’s not suitable (e.g., not eco-friendly enough or duplicate entry), they might reject it – possibly marking it as “Archived” or “Rejected” (we have statuses like archived/flagged in the schema for this purpose

). Admin should also be able to edit the content for clarity or formatting before publishing. This workflow can happen directly in the Sanity CMS (where status is a field) or via a custom admin panel in the app that interfaces with the backend. Initially, using Sanity’s own interface is acceptable since it has the fields and the team can operate there. We will document the moderation guidelines so the process is consistent.
Content Verification and Updates: Many listings will be initially populated by our team (seed data). Each listing has a Verification Status field (unverified vs verified)

. Published listings might start as “unverified” if added by users or scraped from external info, and then an editor can mark “verified” once they have confirmed the eco-friendly claims (e.g., saw certificates or visited the place). This status should be visible internally and possibly to users (with a “verified” label on fully vetted ones). Technical Architecture
Platform Overview
The application is structured as a monolithic web application with a modern Jamstack-like architecture. It consists of: a Next.js front-end (which also handles server-side rendering and API routes), a Sanity.io headless CMS for content, and a MongoDB database for user accounts and other dynamic data. All components are cloud-hosted and integrated via well-defined APIs. The architecture favors server-side rendering (SSR) and static generation for speed and SEO, while using client-side interactivity for dynamic features like filtering. Below is a breakdown of the main technical components and how they interact:
Next.js 15+ Application: The front-end is built on Next.js, utilizing the App Router (which provides an improved structure for pages, layouts, and data fetching). Pages like listing details and city pages will likely be statically generated (SSG) at build time or on-demand when new content is published, because the content doesn’t change too frequently. This yields fast load times and good SEO (pre-rendered HTML). We will use Incremental Static Regeneration (ISR) so that when content in CMS updates, the Next site can revalidate and update those pages after a certain interval or on a webhook trigger. For pages that require real-time data (e.g., search results which might be more dynamic or personalized content), Next.js server-side rendering or API routes will be used. Next.js also provides built-in optimizations like image optimization and bundling which we will leverage. The app is written in TypeScript for type safety across the codebase.
Sanity CMS (Content Backend): Sanity.io is the source of truth for all directory content. We have defined schemas for Listings, City, EcoTag (for sustainability tags), Review, and potentially Blog Post, etc. The Next.js app fetches data from Sanity via its client API (either using Sanity’s JavaScript client or via GROQ queries in getStaticProps/getServerSideProps). Since content is mostly read-heavy (many more reads than writes), Sanity’s CDN and query capabilities will be sufficient. On the write side, content editors use the Sanity Studio (a separate interface) to manage content. For certain user actions like submitting a listing or adding a review, the Next.js app will actually perform writes to Sanity under the hood (e.g., by calling Sanity’s mutation API from an API route). This requires an authenticated service account token for our app to use (securely stored). Example: when a user submits a review, our API route will create a new review document in Sanity and patch the corresponding listing document to reference that review in its reviews array

. We chose Sanity for its flexibility and real-time content capabilities. It also allows us to easily query for combined data, like “get all listings in city X with category Y” which can be done in one query.
Data Model: Key data collections in Sanity include:
City: documents containing city info (name, country, description, sustainabilityScore, highlights, main image)


.
Listing: documents for each venue with fields as detailed earlier (name, slug, city reference, category, descriptions, tags, images, features, contact info possibly, and embedded sub-objects for category-specific info)


. It also contains moderation fields (status, featured, verification)


 and an array of references to Review documents. If a listing is marked featured, we might also maintain a separate list or query in the app for all featured ones to display on home.
EcoTag: a simple reference list of eco-friendly tags (like a taxonomy)

. Listings reference these tags to avoid duplication and to allow filtering by tag easily.
Review: each review is a document with rating, comment, author name, date


. (In the future, we might link reviews to user accounts instead of just storing author name as text, but initially this is how it’s structured in CMS for simplicity.) Reviews are linked to listings as mentioned.
Blog Post: (if using CMS for blog) documents with fields like title, slug, content, author, etc.
We might also have collections for any other structured content like an Events collection in the future or a global settings document (to store things like site-wide settings or featured list IDs).
Sanity allows real-time previews and live updates. For example, if an editor publishes a change, we could use Sanity’s webhook to trigger a revalidation of the Next.js pages so changes go live almost immediately. We will set up these webhooks for critical content types (like Listings and Blog).
User Database (MongoDB): User accounts and authentication data are handled outside of Sanity. We use MongoDB Atlas as our user database

 extend the user schema in Mongo to include a list of favorite listing document IDs (Sanity _id). Resolve to the current slug at read time.
. All password reset/email verification flows will use NextAuth (or custom flows if needed with tokens emailed via our email service).
APIs and Serverless Functions: We will create various API endpoints (using Next.js API routes or the new route handlers in the App directory) to handle form submissions and any complex operations:
A POST /api/contact for the contact form, which sends an email out.
A POST /api/submit-listing for listing suggestions, which will take the form data and create a draft in Sanity via Sanity’s mutation API.
A POST /api/reviews for submitting a review, which writes to Sanity as described.
Possibly a GET /api/search endpoint if we decide to implement search server-side (taking query params for filters and returning results). Alternatively, we might fetch directly from the front-end using Sanity client, but an API gives more control (and hides our Sanity keys). We might do an API route that queries Sanity and returns JSON results of listings for a given filter set.
Any other admin actions (e.g., an endpoint to approve a listing if not using CMS directly).
Each of these endpoints will include necessary security (e.g., anti-CSRF tokens on forms or NextAuth session check for actions that require login). They’ll run in a serverless context on Vercel, scaling as needed with demand.
Third-Party Integrations:
Map Service: Using Leaflet.js for maps with OpenStreetMap tiles means we don’t need a third-party API key (OSM is open). We will however likely use a free OSM tiles provider or self-hosted tiles if needed. The map component in our Next.js app will load the Leaflet library and render maps client-side. Coordinates for listings come from our data (as discussed, either geocoded beforehand or input manually). If we need geocoding (converting addresses to lat/long), we might use a service like Google Geocoding API or OpenCage during data entry (not something users do, but maybe an offline script like the migration one has done). For dynamic user input addresses (like if owners add a listing with an address), we might call a geocoding API in the submission process to get coordinates to store. This is an implementation detail – initial dataset likely already has coordinates as we saw placeholders for location.
Map Service (production):
- Use Leaflet with a commercial-grade tiles provider (MapTiler, Mapbox, or Stadia).
- Manage API keys via environment variables; restrict by domain/referrer, rotate periodically, and include required provider attribution.
- Do NOT proxy tiles through our server. Provider-side quotas/limits apply; our server-side rate limiting only covers our own API routes (e.g., geocoding/search).
- Default OSM tile servers are dev-only. In production, use a paid/free-tier plan or self-hosted tiles if we explicitly take that on.

Implementation notes:
- Dynamically import Leaflet on the client to avoid SSR errors in Next.js App Router.
- Provide a graceful “map unavailable” fallback if keys are missing/misconfigured.
- Coordinates are sourced from persisted data (pre-geocoded or operator-input).

If geocoding is required, use Google Geocoding API or OpenCage during data entry or in the submission workflow to persist lat/long. Initial datasets likely already include coordinates (placeholders exist for location).

Testing: We will use Playwright for end-to-end testing
 and possibly Jest for unit tests. The PRD expects quality assurance: the refactored product should be thoroughly tested to avoid regressions. Critical flows like search, login, and submissions will have automated tests. This ensures that as we iterate, we maintain reliability.
Data Flow Summary
To illustrate a typical interaction: when a user visits a city page, Next.js will fetch the list of published listings for that city from Sanity (either at build time or on request). The data comes via a GROQ query filtering by city reference and status = published. Next builds the HTML and serves it. When a logged-in user submits a review on a listing page, the browser calls our /api/reviews route with the review data; the server (Next API) validates the session, then creates a new document in Sanity for the review and updates the listing’s review reference array. Sanity instantly stores it, and our API returns success. The next time someone fetches that listing (or via revalidation), the new review appears. If the review requires approval, we might mark it differently and filter unapproved ones out of the query. Similarly, for adding a listing: user fills form -> /api/submit-listing -> Next API writes to Sanity (status pending) -> admin later changes status to published. Because of ISR or webhooks, that new listing will then be included on the site. This coherent flow ensures content and user contributions are integrated seamlessly.
Security & Compliance
Security is critical since we handle user accounts and content. Key measures include:
Authentication Security: Use industry-standard practices via NextAuth. Passwords are never stored in plain text (NextAuth uses bcrypt or similar hashing). We will enforce a strong password policy (minimum length, complexity) and possibly have rate limiting on login attempts to prevent brute force attacks. Session tokens will be secure, HTTP-only cookies. We’ll also consider using HTTPS everywhere (Vercel provides SSL by default).
Authorization & Data Protection: Only authorized roles can perform sensitive actions (like moderation or editing content). The API routes will double-check user roles in the server (never trust just front-end to hide admin features). For example, the submit-listing endpoint can be open to all (even guests, if we allow suggestion without account), but the approve-listing endpoint will require admin. Access to the CMS itself is also secured by Sanity’s role system (only our team or invited contributors can log in to Sanity Studio).
Validation & Sanitization: All user-provided data (form inputs for listings, reviews, contact messages) will be validated and sanitized. We will use libraries like Zod or built-in validation to ensure no malicious script can be injected (e.g., strip out HTML tags from reviews, limit length of inputs). This prevents XSS and keeps content clean. On the display side, we will carefully render user content to avoid any script execution (e.g., reviews might be plain text, or if we allow a bit of formatting in certain fields, we’ll use a safe renderer).
Dependency and Platform Security: We will keep Next.js and dependencies up-to-date. The refactor will remove any known vulnerable packages. Vercel and Mongo Atlas are responsible for the underlying infrastructure security (patching, etc.), which is an advantage of using those managed services. We will perform some security testing (maybe using static analysis or dependency audits, which  provides) and consider a penetration test if feasible before launch.
Privacy Compliance: We will ensure our use of user data complies with privacy policies. We’ll obtain user consent where needed (for analytics cookies, perhaps via a cookie banner). Users can request deletion of their data, which our team will handle by removing their account from the DB and anonymizing their reviews. We won’t share user data with third parties. These principles will be stated in the Privacy Policy, and our implementation will follow them.

For instance, if search becomes heavy, integrate an external search service (like Algolia/Meili) to offload search queries and provide instant results. If traffic is very high, consider using a CDN for the API responses or optimizing database indexes, etc. Also monitor and optimize build times (if content grows significantly, static generation might slow down; using ISR and caching becomes important).
Multi-Tenancy / Expansion: Prepare the system to handle multiple countries or a broader geographic range if expansion is planned. This could involve adding a Country model, adjusting the navigation (perhaps listing countries then cities), and ensuring content structure supports it. It might also entail translating the site to other languages for non-English users. These are major efforts and would be done when the strategy calls for expanding beyond Thailand. The PRD acknowledges this as a future possibility but not immediate.
Monetization Features: Once the platform has a solid user base, we can introduce ways to generate revenue ethically. Some options: featured placements (venues pay to be “featured” or higher in search – we’d need to mark them as sponsored), affiliate links (for example, link to booking platforms for accommodations and earn commission), or a paid membership for users that offers perks (though in this context, perhaps not likely). We will design any monetization such that it doesn’t compromise user trust (i.e., clearly label sponsored content, and maintain neutrality in normal listings vs paid). Payment integration (Stripe, etc.) would be needed for some of these – which would be a new aspect to add.
Mobile App or PWA: Evaluate building a Progressive Web App experience – the site is already responsive, but making it a PWA could allow offline access to saved info or push notifications for new content. Alternatively, if user demand is high, we might consider developing a companion mobile app for iOS/Android in the future, reusing much of the web logic. This is outside the web PRD’s immediate scope, but it’s an eventual consideration.
Advanced Admin Tools: Build more sophisticated admin dashboards and perhaps integrate analytics for admin view (e.g., see site usage stats, see which listings are most popular, etc.). Also implement bulk management features in CMS (like flagging multiple outdated listings). We may also incorporate a content versioning or audit log to track changes (Sanity has history per document already). If needed, implement backup routines for the CMS data (though Sanity is managed, it’s good to periodically export data for safe-keeping).
AI Integration (Exploratory): As a cutting-edge idea, possibly integrate AI to enhance user experience – for example, an AI chatbot that can answer questions like “What’s the best area in Bangkok for digital nomads interested in sustainability?” by drawing from our content. Or use AI to analyze user reviews sentiment, or auto-suggest tags for new listings based on description. This is not a confirmed requirement, but something to keep in mind if it aligns with the product vision later (since 2025 tech trends often involve such features).
Phase 3 items will be prioritized based on user feedback and business direction after phases 1 and 2 are live. Not all may be implemented at once; we will choose the features that provide the greatest value relative to their cost. Importantly, by Phase 3 the product should already be solid – these are enhancements to maintain growth and competitive edge.
Logical Dependency & Development Priorities
The development plan should follow a logical sequence to build a usable product early and then layer on enhancements:
Foundation Setup: Start with setting up the design system, project architecture (Next.js, CMS schema, database connections). This includes defining all data schemas in Sanity and ensuring we can query them, and implementing NextAuth basics. Much of this was already scaffolded in the existing project (monorepo, NextAuth infra, CMS config)


. We first ensure that baseline is working (e.g., can we fetch a listing from CMS and render it, can we sign up a user in dev environment). This foundation is necessary before building features on top.
Static Pages & Content Display: Implement the listing pages (city page, listing detail page) and static content pages (About, etc.) early. By focusing on read-only content first, we get the main site flow visible. It’s important to see the listings and navigation in place. This also includes integrating the map component on listing pages and verifying the performance. Once we have a few example listings showing up correctly, it’s a big milestone – we have something “usable” (even if not feature-complete).
Search & Filtering: Next, implement the search functionality because it ties together the content. Having search working means a user can find the listing pages we built. Search involves both front-end UI and hooking to backend queries. It’s logically dependent on content being in place, and it will likely involve refining how we query the CMS. Doing this early (in Phase 1) ensures that the data model supports the queries we need (for example, if we find we need an additional index or field to filter by tag efficiently, we can adjust the CMS schema or data structures before going too far).
User Accounts & Auth: After the core browsing experience is solid, we implement authentication. This unlocks the ability to then build user-specific features. The dependency logic here is that reviews and favorites require user accounts, so we must have auth done before enabling those features. Implementing auth earlier (beginning of Phase 2) is wise so that we can test protected routes and role logic gradually as we add those sections.
Interactive Feature Implementation: Once auth is in, we proceed to build reviews and favorites (and any other logged-in features like profile management). Reviews might come first as it’s a key community feature – we build the form, storage, display, then test that pipeline. Favorites is a bit simpler (just toggling a relation) so it can follow. The listing submission form can be developed in parallel or after reviews, since it touches different parts (it’s more about writing to CMS rather than reading). We’ll likely do favorites last among these, as it’s least complex and somewhat independent (just needs user login, which by then is done).
Moderation Tools & Admin: As soon as we have any user-generated content flowing (reviews or submissions), we should implement at least minimal admin tools to manage them. That might be as easy as giving admins instructions to use Sanity Studio to monitor new docs. But if building a custom admin UI, it should come after those features are working, focusing on efficiency for the team. The logical dependency is: user content flows first, then admin UI to manage it. (We can moderate manually in DB for initial testing, but should not launch without giving admins the means to moderate easily.)
Polish & Non-functional Requirements: Throughout development, but especially towards end of each phase, ensure we address non-functional aspects: performance optimizations (e.g., test page load with many listings), security audits, and cross-browser/device testing. These tasks depend on having the features in place to test. So after implementing major features in a phase, allocate time to polish UI details, fix any accessibility issues found, and optimize queries or code where needed.
Testing and QA: Write tests as we implement features (e.g., unit tests for util functions, integration tests for API routes). But a final regression test before release of each phase is needed. For example, before launching Phase 1, test all pages for broken links, test search with various inputs, etc. Before launching Phase 2, test all new user flows (account creation, posting a review, etc.). This sequential testing ensures each layer of features doesn’t break earlier ones.
By following this chain, we ensure that at each step we have a working product that could be demonstrated or even used in a limited capacity. We “get as quickly as possible to something usable/visible front end that works” – which is essentially Phase 1 delivering a browsable directory. Then we carefully add the layers of interaction in Phase 2 without overwhelming the development process. This logical sequencing also helps with team focus: content and frontend first, then auth, then contributions, then extras.
Risks and Mitigations
Developing and launching this platform comes with several risks. We identify key risks and how we plan to mitigate them:
Content Sufficiency & Quality Risk: The value of the directory depends on having enough listings with reliable information. If we launch with too few listings or incomplete data, users may not find it useful. Mitigation: Before launch, ensure a critical mass of listings in the main cities (e.g., at least 10-20 good listings per major city, covering various categories). Leverage manual research and partnerships to populate data. Also clearly indicate if the site is still growing (so early users understand more content is coming). Use the verification status fields to be transparent about which entries have been confirmed

. Having a blog with useful content can also keep early users engaged even if some directory content is still filling out.
User Adoption Risk: There is a risk that digital nomads might not discover or use the platform, either due to lack of awareness or preference for existing solutions (like generic travel sites or Facebook groups). Mitigation: Implement strong SEO practices so that search engines surface our site for relevant queries (“eco-friendly co-working Thailand”, “digital nomad Chiang Mai” etc.). The blog content will help attract organic traffic. Additionally, plan a marketing strategy: share the site in digital nomad communities, possibly partner with sustainable travel bloggers or influencers for exposure. Once users arrive, ensure the value proposition is clear (the homepage should clearly state it’s for eco-conscious nomads). A clean UX and visible community contributions (reviews) will encourage them to trust and return.
Development Scope Creep: The scope of features is broad; there’s a risk of trying to do too much at once, causing delays or quality issues. Mitigation: Stick to the phased approach. Clearly delineate what is in Phase 1 vs Phase 2. If during development a feature is taking too long or proving complex (e.g., real-time filtering might be tricky), be willing to simplify or push it to the next phase to keep the schedule. Regularly review progress against the PRD to avoid feature drift. The PRD’s detailed breakdown can serve as a checklist to prevent off-track developments.
Technical Integration Challenges: There could be unforeseen issues integrating Next.js with Sanity, NextAuth, or other libraries (e.g., a compatibility issue with Next.js 15 and some plugin). Mitigation: Use known stable versions and read documentation/examples for similar setups. We have a Development Guide (in docs) to follow best practices. Write integration tests early (for example, test a round-trip of data: create a dummy listing in Sanity, see if Next can fetch and render it). Having a staging environment to test end-to-end before production is also important – Vercel preview deployments can serve this purpose.
Performance Scaling Risk: As content or traffic grows, pages might slow down (especially if lots of client-side JS for maps or filtering). Mitigation: Use performance best practices from the start: SSR/SSG content, paginate lists if they become large, utilize caching (browser and CDN caching for static assets and maybe API responses). Monitor performance (using web vitals analytics) and be ready to optimize (like moving heavy computations server-side or employing caching strategies). Because we plan for expansion, choosing scalable solutions like cloud DB and a headless CMS was intentional. We can also consider load testing for anticipated traffic to ensure the architecture holds up.
Security & Abuse: With user-generated content and accounts, there’s risk of spam, fake accounts, or malicious content injection. Mitigation: Implement the validations and moderation as described. Use CAPTCHA for public forms (submission, contact)

 to deter bots. Monitor logs for any suspicious activity. Keep dependencies updated to patch known vulnerabilities. Also enforce secure headers (Next can set things like Content Security Policy to prevent XSS, etc.). Regularly back up the database and CMS content so that if something goes wrong (like data corruption or an admin error), we can recover.
User Trust & Moderation: If unmoderated, user reviews or submissions could reduce trust (e.g., a venue getting falsely accused in a review, or someone tries to list a place that isn’t truly eco-friendly, undermining our credibility). Mitigation: Establish clear community guidelines for reviews and content. Have an active moderation plan – at least one admin should periodically check new entries and reviews. We’ve built tools like status flags and an easy way to mark content as “flagged”

 which we can use if something needs to be removed pending a decision. Respond to issues quickly (e.g., if a business contacts us to dispute a review or ask for removal, handle it promptly to maintain goodwill). In essence, commit resources (time) to moderation as an ongoing operational task, not just a technical feature.
Timeline Risks: If this refactor is time-sensitive (the PRD mention of a weekend sprint was internal

, but for full project likely we have more time), missing deadlines can be an issue. Mitigation: Prioritize critical features for each phase and potentially launch in beta to gather feedback. Use the roadmap to guide development, and don’t let secondary features delay the core launch. If necessary, reduce scope of Phase 1 to make a launch date (for example, if blog content isn’t ready, it can launch slightly later, that’s fine). Also ensure to allocate time for testing and content entry, which are often underestimated.
By anticipating these risks and following the mitigations, we aim to deliver a robust product with minimal hiccups. Regular reviews, user testing (maybe get a few friendly nomads to try a beta), and agile adjustments will further help in managing risk throughout development and launch.
